\begin{solution}{ex:localrx:1}
Directly:
\begin{align*}
\text{outer} &= (2\times15 + 1)^2 = 31^2 = 961\\
\text{guard} &= (2\times3 + 1)^2 = 7^2 = 49\\
n_{\text{ring}} &= 961 - 49 = 912 .
\end{align*}
With \cref{eq:localrx:ring}: $4\times(15 - 3)\times(15 + 3 + 1) = 4\times12\times19 = 912$.
At a corner only rows and columns $0$ to $15$ of the outer window lie inside the image, a
$16\times16 = 256$ square, and the guard keeps rows and columns $0$ to $3$, $4\times4 = 16$. The corner
ring has $256 - 16 = 240$ pixels, about a quarter of 912.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:localrx:2}
With $i = 0$ the ring is all 24 pixels except the centre: the 16 border pixels (eight 20s, eight 22s)
and the 8 inner neighbours (four 20s, four 22s), so twelve 20s and twelve 22s.
\begin{align*}
\mu &= \frac{12\times20 + 12\times22}{24} = \frac{240 + 264}{24} = \frac{504}{24} = 21\\
\textstyle\sum(x - \mu)^2 &= 12\times1 + 12\times1 = 24\\
\sigma^2 &= 24/23 = 1.0435\\
\text{RX} &= \frac{(25 - 21)^2}{1.0435} = \frac{16}{1.0435} = 15.33 .
\end{align*}
A one-pixel target scores as high without a guard as \cref{we:localrx:guard}'s target did with one
(slightly higher, because 24 background pixels estimate the variance a little better than 16). The
guard is needed when the target, or the blur around it, extends beyond the pixel under test; its size
should match the largest target you expect.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:localrx:3}
Sum of the real values: $0.20 + 0.22 + 0.24 = 0.66$. Correct mean $0.66/3 = 0.22$; dividing by the
padded length gives $0.66/6 = 0.11$, exactly half, because half the slots are padding.

Variance with divisor $n - 1 = 2$: deviations $-0.02, 0, 0.02$; squares $0.0004, 0, 0.0004$; sum
$0.0008$; $\sigma^2 = 0.0008/2 = 0.0004$. Score of $0.30$:
\[
\frac{(0.30 - 0.22)^2}{0.0004} = \frac{0.0064}{0.0004} = 16 .
\]
(With the wrong mean 0.11 the score would be $(0.19)^2/\sigma^2_{\text{wrong}}$ with a variance also
corrupted by the padded zeros: a meaningless number at exactly the pixels near edges.)
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:localrx:4}
$\mat{W}_n = \diag(1/\sqrt2, 1/\sqrt{0.5}) = \diag(0.7071, 1.4142)$. Entry $(i,j)$ of
$\mat{W}_n\Cov\mat{W}_n$ is $\Sigma_{ij}$ times the $i$-th and $j$-th diagonal entries:
\begin{align*}
(1,1):&\quad 3\times0.7071\times0.7071 = 3\times0.5 = 1.5\\
(1,2):&\quad 1\times0.7071\times1.4142 = 1\\
(2,2):&\quad 1.5\times1.4142\times1.4142 = 1.5\times2 = 3 .
\end{align*}
Trace $4.5$, determinant $1.5\times3 - 1 = 3.5$; $\lambda^2 - 4.5\lambda + 3.5 = 0$ gives
$\lambda = (4.5 \pm \sqrt{20.25 - 14})/2 = (4.5 \pm 2.5)/2$, so $\lambda_1 = 3.5$ and $\lambda_2 = 1$.
The first MNF component has $1 + \text{SNR} = 3.5$, SNR $= 2.5$; the second is pure noise.

Eigenvector for 3.5: $(1.5 - 3.5)v_1 + v_2 = 0$, so $v_2 = 2v_1$, $\vect{v}_1 = (1, 2)/\sqrt5$. In band
space $\vect{a}_1 = \mat{W}_n\vect{v}_1 = (0.7071, 2.8284)/\sqrt5 = (0.316, 1.265)$: band 2, the cleaner
band (noise variance 0.5 against 2), is weighted four times as heavily. Check: the signal covariance is
$\Cov - \Cov_n = \begin{psmallmatrix} 1 & 1\\ 1 & 1\end{psmallmatrix}$, so
$\vect{a}_1\T\Cov_s\vect{a}_1 = (0.316 + 1.265)^2 = 1.581^2 = 2.5$, and the noise variance is
$2\times0.316^2 + 0.5\times1.265^2 = 2\times0.1 + 0.5\times1.6 = 0.2 + 0.8 = 1$: SNR $2.5$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:localrx:5}
Let $\vect{d} = \vect{x} - \muv$ and $\vect{y} = \mat{A}\vect{d}$. The mean of $\vect{y}$ is
$\mat{A}\times\vect{0} = \vect{0}$ and its covariance is $\mat{A}\Cov\mat{A}\T$. Its Mahalanobis distance is
\begin{align*}
\vect{y}\T(\mat{A}\Cov\mat{A}\T)\inv\vect{y}
&= \vect{d}\T\mat{A}\T\,(\mat{A}\T)\inv\Cov\inv\mat{A}\inv\,\mat{A}\vect{d}
  && (\mat{A}\vect{d})\T = \vect{d}\T\mat{A}\T;\ (\mat{P}\mat{Q}\mat{R})\inv = \mat{R}\inv\mat{Q}\inv\mat{P}\inv\\
&= \vect{d}\T\Cov\inv\vect{d}
  && \mat{A}\T(\mat{A}\T)\inv = \mat{I},\ \mat{A}\inv\mat{A} = \mat{I}.
\end{align*}
The full MNF transform is such an $\mat{A}$ (rows $\vect{a}_k\T = \vect{v}_k\T\mat{W}_n$, a product of an
orthogonal matrix and an invertible whitening matrix), so full MNF-RX equals plain RX.

MNF-RX helps because it keeps only the first 10 components. (1) Numerically, the discarded directions
include those whose estimates are dominated by noise and rounding; the 10 kept have eigenvalues
$1 + \text{SNR}$ well above the noise level, so the $10\times10$ covariance is well conditioned and well
estimated. (2) Statistically, each discarded near-noise component would add a $\chi^2_1$ term to every
score and nothing to a target's, so dropping them shrinks the background spread $\sqrt{2C}$ against which
a target must stand out.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{ex:localrx:6}
A symmetric $30\times30$ matrix has $30$ diagonal entries and $(30\times30 - 30)/2 = 435$ distinct
off-diagonal ones: $30 + 435 = 465 = 30\times31/2$ numbers. The global sample of 200,000 pixels gives
$200{,}000/465 = 430$ pixels per number. A local covariance from a 3280-pixel ring would have
$3280/465 = 7.1$ pixels per number, far fewer, and every pixel would need its own $30\times30$ solve.
That is one reason PCA-RX and the residual score take only the \emph{mean} from the ring and keep one
global covariance.
\end{solution}
